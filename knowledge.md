## MIST 2090 course overview
MIST 2090: Introduction to Information Systems in Business teaches students how information technology enables change in organizations. The course covers business value, business processes, data, artificial intelligence, project management, cybersecurity, data visualization, interface design, and digital innovation. Students gain hands-on experience with business technologies and learn how technology can be used to solve organizational challenges. The course combines conceptual material with practical work using Microsoft Excel and other business tools. 【1-843027】

## MIST 2090 grading breakdown
Grades in MIST 2090 are based on several categories. AI and Project Exercises are worth 5%, Class Participation through Top Hat is worth 15%, the Project Portfolio is worth 10%, LinkedIn Learning assignments are worth 5%, eLC Check-Ins are worth 10%, three exams together are worth 30%, and the Final Exam is worth 25%. Students earn an A with 92.5% or higher and an A- with 89.5% to 92.49%. The course uses a traditional plus/minus grading scale. 【1-843027】

## MIST 2090 exams and final exam
The course includes three exams and one cumulative final exam. Exams cover both conceptual course content and Microsoft Excel skills. Excel content is cumulative because later topics build on earlier material. The final exam is fully cumulative and covers material from the entire semester. Students are expected to take exams in person unless alternate arrangements have been approved by the instructor. Missed exams with valid excuses must be completed within five working days after the excused absence ends. 【1-843027】

MIST 2090 uses Top Hat to track class participation. Students participate in lecture activities using either the Top Hat mobile app or a computer. # Introductory Risk Management and Insurance (RMI) Notes
These notes are introductory educational summaries for college students. They are not legal, insurance-coverage, or financial advice. Definitions can vary by context and policy wording.

## RMI 1: What risk management means
Risk management is the process of identifying and analyzing exposures to loss and choosing actions to reduce their likelihood or financial impact. It helps individuals and organizations make informed decisions about uncertain events. Insurance is one possible part of risk management, not the whole discipline. A basic process is to identify exposures, analyze their likelihood and consequences, select a response, and monitor the result. Source: IRMI, Risk Management (https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/risk-management); Insurance Information Institute, Risk Management Basics (https://www.iii.org/publication/insuring-your-business-small-business-owners-guide-to-insurance/risk-management/risk-management-basics).

## RMI 2: Pure risk and speculative risk
Pure risk involves the possibility of loss or no loss, but no opportunity for gain; examples can include fire damage, illness, or theft. Speculative risk includes the possibility of loss, no change, or gain; investing in a business venture is a common example. Traditional insurance is generally designed for fortuitous pure risks rather than speculative opportunities. Do not assume every pure risk is automatically insurable; policy terms and insurability conditions still matter. Source: IRMI, Pure Risk (https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/pure-risk); IRMI, Financial Risk Management (https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/financial-risk-management).

## RMI 3: Risk identification and analysis
Risk identification means recognizing events or conditions that could cause harm or financial loss. Risk analysis considers how likely a loss is and how severe its consequences could be. A simple introductory method is to list a hazard or exposure, describe a possible loss, estimate likelihood, estimate severity, and prioritize the exposure. A risk that is unlikely but catastrophic may deserve attention alongside a frequent, lower-cost risk. Source: IRMI, Risk Management (https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/risk-management); Insurance Information Institute, Risk Management Basics (https://www.iii.org/publication/insuring-your-business-small-business-owners-guide-to-insurance/risk-management/risk-management-basics).

## RMI 4: Risk control and loss prevention
Risk control consists of actions intended to avoid losses or reduce their frequency or severity. Examples include avoiding a hazardous activity, installing smoke alarms, maintaining equipment, using protective equipment, and training employees. Loss prevention focuses on reducing the chance that a loss occurs; loss reduction focuses on limiting the size or consequences of a loss after an event begins. Risk control does not guarantee that losses will never happen. Source: Insurance Information Institute, Risk Management Basics (https://www.iii.org/publication/insuring-your-business-small-business-owners-guide-to-insurance/risk-management/risk-management-basics).

## RMI 5: Risk financing, retention, and transfer
Risk financing concerns how an individual or organization will pay for losses. Retention means keeping some or all of the financial consequences of a risk, such as paying small predictable losses from available funds or using a deductible. Transfer shifts specified financial consequences to another party through a contract; insurance is a common example, subject to exclusions, limits, and conditions. Risk control changes the likelihood or severity of loss, while risk financing addresses how losses will be funded. Source: IRMI, Risk Management (https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/risk-management); NAIC, How Does Insurance Work? (https://content.naic.org/consumer/how-does-insurance-work).

## RMI 6: How insurance works
Insurance is a contractual method of managing financial risk. A policyholder pays a premium, and the insurer agrees to provide specified financial protection for covered events under the policy terms. Insurance does not cover every possible event or every dollar of every loss. Coverage depends on the policy's insuring agreement, exclusions, limits, conditions, and applicable law. A policyholder should read the policy and ask the insurer or state insurance department about unclear coverage. Source: NAIC, How Does Insurance Work? (https://content.naic.org/consumer/how-does-insurance-work).

## RMI 7: Premiums, deductibles, and claims
A premium is the payment made to keep an insurance policy in force. A deductible is the amount the policyholder pays toward a covered loss before the insurer pays its share, as specified by the policy. A claim is a request for the insurer to pay benefits for a loss or service that may be covered. These terms are related but not interchangeable: the premium buys the coverage, the deductible is a cost-sharing amount when a covered claim occurs, and the claim is the request for payment. Exact arrangements vary by policy type. Source: NAIC, How Does Insurance Work? (https://content.naic.org/consumer/how-does-insurance-work); NAIC, Glossary of Insurance Terms (https://content.naic.org/glossary-insurance-terms).

## RMI 8: Underwriting and rating
Underwriting is the process an insurer uses to evaluate an applicant or exposure and decide whether to offer coverage and under what terms. Rating is the process of determining the price charged for coverage. Insurers may consider different factors depending on the line of insurance, state law, and the product. Underwriting and rating are related but distinct: underwriting evaluates and classifies risk, while rating determines the premium. Students should not assume a factor is used or permitted in every state or every type of insurance. Source: NAIC, Glossary of Insurance Terms (https://content.naic.org/glossary-insurance-terms); NAIC, Auto Insurance (https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/auto-insurance).

## RMI 9: Insurance policy coverage and exclusions
An insurance policy is a contract that describes the coverage provided and the obligations of the parties. Covered events are limited by the policy language, including definitions, exclusions, conditions, and limits. A loss can occur without being covered, and a policy may cover only part of a loss. When evaluating a hypothetical claim, identify the relevant coverage, check exclusions and conditions, and avoid promising payment without reviewing the actual policy and facts. Source: NAIC, How Does Insurance Work? (https://content.naic.org/consumer/how-does-insurance-work).

## RMI 10: Common types of personal insurance
Common personal insurance categories include auto, homeowners, renters, health, life, disability, dental, vision, pet, and travel insurance. Each type addresses different exposures and has different coverage terms. For example, auto insurance may address vehicle damage or liability, while renters insurance commonly addresses a tenant's personal property and certain liability exposures. The exact coverage depends on the policy; a category name alone does not establish that a specific event is covered. Source: NAIC, How Does Insurance Work? (https://content.naic.org/consumer/how-does-insurance-work); NAIC, Health Insurance (https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/health-insurance).

## RMI 11: Adverse selection
Adverse selection is a situation in which people with a higher-than-average likelihood of loss are more likely to seek or obtain greater insurance coverage than people with lower expected losses. It can affect the composition of an insurance pool and the insurer's expected claims. Insurers use underwriting, eligibility rules, pricing, and other methods—subject to applicable law—to evaluate and manage this issue. Source: NAIC, Glossary of Insurance Terms (https://content.naic.org/glossary-insurance-terms).

## RMI 12: Insurance pooling and the law of large numbers
Insurance pooling combines the premiums and risks of many policyholders so that losses of the few can be funded by contributions from the many. The law of large numbers is a statistical principle: as the number of similar, independent exposure units increases, actual average results tend to become more stable around expected results. This helps insurers estimate aggregate losses, but it does not mean an individual loss is predictable or that every risk is insurable. Source: NAIC, How Does Insurance Work? (https://content.naic.org/consumer/how-does-insurance-work); NAIC, Glossary of Insurance Terms (https://content.naic.org/glossary-insurance-terms).

## RMI 13: The role of claims adjustment
A claim is a request for payment under an insurance policy. Claims handling generally involves reporting the event, gathering relevant documentation, reviewing the facts and policy terms, evaluating the loss, and determining whether and how much the policy covers. An adjuster investigates claims and may recommend settlement options based on the damage and applicable policy. A claim submission does not guarantee that the insurer will pay; coverage depends on the contract and facts. Source: NAIC, How Does Insurance Work? (https://content.naic.org/consumer/how-does-insurance-work); NAIC, Glossary of Insurance Terms (https://content.naic.org/glossary-insurance-terms).

## RMI 14: Insurance regulation and consumer questions
Insurance is regulated at the state level in the United States. State departments of insurance provide consumer information and help address questions or complaints about insurers and policies. Because rules and coverage requirements can vary by state and product, students should verify current requirements with the relevant state insurance department and consult the actual policy. RMIBot provides educational explanations and should not make a legal determination or promise that a claim is covered. Source: NAIC, How Does Insurance Work? (https://content.naic.org/consumer/how-does-insurance-work).
